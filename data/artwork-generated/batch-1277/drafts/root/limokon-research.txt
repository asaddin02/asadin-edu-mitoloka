
=== limokon (lengkap-informasi, batch-019)
Limokon | tier rich | bird | jenis hewan mitos | philippine-folklore
Ringkas: The limokon is an omen bird of the Mandaya, Bagobo and Manobo of Mindanao, a wild pigeon whose call is believed to carry warnings from the spirits.
Kemampuan: prophecy — Its call conveys the spirits' warnings or encouragement; its sound is read as a good or bad sign for journeys and undertakings. [limokon-c04, limokon-c14, limokon-c17, limokon-c18]
limokon-c01 (traditional-belief): The Mandaya, Bagobo and Manobo of Mindanao believed the limokon an omen bird, like the tigmamanukan among the Tagalog of Luzon.
limokon-c02 (historical-record): Francisco Combes (1667) recorded that the people firmly believed in omens, including from birds like the limocon.
limokon-c03 (scholarly-interpretation): The term limokon likely named various wild pigeons in the Philippine languages, some thought magical or omen birds.
limokon-c04 (traditional-belief): According to Fay Cooper Cole (1913), spirits commonly speak to mortals through the limokon's call; all understand and respect its warnings, and a sale is stopped if it warns.
limokon-c05 (traditional-belief): If the limokon calls when a person is on the trail, he at once doubles his fist and thrusts it in the direction from which the warning comes.
limokon-c06 (traditional-belief): When it is not clear where the note came from, the traveler looks to the right side; if he sees strong, sturdy trees there, all is well, but if they are cut or weak, he should take great care to avoid impending danger.
limokon-c07 (traditional-belief): Asked why one should look only to the right, an old man replied that the right side belongs to you, while the left side is bad and belongs to someone else.
limokon-c08 (traditional-belief): If the dove limokan (a variant spelling of limokon) calls on the left side of the trail, the party will refuse to proceed unless another limokan answers from the right side of the path.
limokon-c09 (traditional-belief): The close relationship of the limokon to the Mandaya is given as the reason its calls are heeded so closely; a traveler hearing the cooing of this bird at once doubles his fist and points it toward the sound.
limokon-c10 (traditional-belief): If the fist points to the left, in front or behind, the Mandaya knows the omen bird is warning him of danger or failure, and he delays or gives up his mission.
limokon-c11 (traditional-belief): After about two hours of work, the call of the limokon was heard to the left of the owner; the men at once gathered their tools and left, explaining it was useless to plant there because the limokon had warned that rats would eat any crop grown on that spot.
limokon-c12 (traditional-belief): The people make no offerings to this bird and do not regard it as a spirit but as a messenger from the spirit world; the old men were certain that anyone who molested one of these birds would die.
limokon-c13 (traditional-belief): Some informants stated that this is the name given to the first man and woman, who emerged from the limokon eggs.
limokon-c14 (traditional-belief): The limokon warns or encourages the traveler, while certain acts of the individual, such as sneezing, are seen as warnings from unseen beings.
limokon-c15 (historical-record): Similar beliefs held by the Tagalog were mentioned by Juan de Plasencia in 1589.
limokon-c16 (scholarly-interpretation): A footnote in this book gives the name Li-mo-kon with the scientific name Phabitreron brevirostris Tweedale, generally called fruit dove.
limokon-c17 (traditional-belief): This homely, inert bird is considered the harbinger of good and evil, and is consulted at the start of every journey and undertaking where its prophetic voice can be heard.
limokon-c18 (traditional-belief): If its cry forebodes ill, the undertaking is discontinued however urgent it may be; if the cry presages good, the project is taken up or continued with renewed assurance and a glad heart.
limokon-c19 (religious-tradition): The bird is regarded as the envoy of the deities and its voice as a divine message.
limokon-c20 (traditional-belief): The omen bird is never killed, because killing it would bring down great misfortune.
limokon-c21 (traditional-belief): The cage is usually hung from the roof under the eaves outside the wall, and the turtledove is kept for religious purposes.
limokon-c22 (traditional-belief): The prospective traveler places a little sweet potato or banana in the cage and asks the captive bird to sing to its companions in the woods so that they in turn may sing to him the song of success and safe return.
limokon-c23 (traditional-belief): Maxey states exactly the opposite for the Mandaya of Cateel: the right side is bad and the left good.
